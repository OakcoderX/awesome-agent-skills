# Research and empirical-argument adapter

Review what the supplied evidence can support and how to test the strongest remaining uncertainty.

## Map claim to design

Identify the population, exposure or intervention, comparison, outcome, unit of analysis, timeframe, and proposed scope. Distinguish descriptive, predictive, causal, mechanistic, and normative claims.

For each material claim, connect it to the exact result or source. Keep a reported result separate from your interpretation. Missing or inaccessible methods limit the conclusion.

## Attack the inference

Choose relevant alternatives rather than listing every possible bias:

- selection and baseline differences;
- confounding, reverse causality, regression to the mean, and secular trends;
- attrition, missing data, measurement shifts, and dependence between observations;
- multiple comparisons, selective reporting, and mismatch between the analyzed and claimed population.

For causal claims, ask what identifies the counterfactual. A before/after difference or correlation alone is not identification. A randomized label does not settle allocation, compliance, interference, missingness, or analysis problems.

Do not infer statistical significance, effect uncertainty, or statistical power from a point estimate alone. Do not fabricate a p-value or confidence interval. Inspect denominators and distinguish percentage-point change from relative change.

## Improve the claim or the study

Prefer the smallest correction that makes the claim defensible: narrow the wording, disclose an alternative, inspect the missing method, reanalyze supplied data, or plan a better comparison. Explain what result would challenge the preferred explanation.

Specify the estimand and key controls for a proposed test. If raw data or expertise is missing, state what cannot be calculated. Never imply that a proposed experiment, code sketch, or simulated sample has been run.

Use current primary sources when an external factual assertion matters. This adapter is not peer review, ethics approval, or clinical advice. Keep human-subject and other high-stakes decisions with the relevant qualified process.
